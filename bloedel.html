Bloedel Reserve by Water

The finest garden in the Pacific Northwest sits across the water from the downtown piers, and the people the city is named for have kept the other half of Seattle's story on the far shore of Agate Passage for ten thousand years. Most of Seattle has never stood in either place.

On the north end of Bainbridge Island, a timber baron's estate became Bloedel Reserve: 140 acres of forest garden, not a flower show. Prentice Bloedel, who spent his career turning a clear-cut empire toward reforestation, and Virginia Bloedel built a Western expression of Japanese quiet on logged land: a Kubota Japanese garden without lanterns, the largest public moss garden in the country, and a 200-foot reflection pool where they are buried. Garden writers place it among the great landscapes of North America. Across Agate Pass sits Suquamish, on the Port Madison Reservation, where d'suq'wub, Old Man House, the greatest longhouse on Puget Sound, stood at the water's edge until a government agent burned it in 1870, and where Chief Seattle is buried within sight of the skyline of the city that carries his name. These two places hold the region's whole story between them, garden and longhouse, and the water in front of them was the original road. Almost nobody arrives the way everyone once did. Burnham Civic is working on that, and the operation file is for members.
