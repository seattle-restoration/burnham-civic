The Federal Property Series

Every federal holding in Seattle, who has it, and what happens to it next. The list below is public. The files are for MEMBERS.

The federal government is one of the largest landowners in this city and almost nobody tracks what it does with the land. When it lets a property go, the law sends it to the city first. Title V of the McKinney-Vento Act screens every surplus federal property for homeless-assistance use before it can be sold, the public benefit conveyance rules then offer it to state and local government at a discount or at no cost, and closed military land moves under the 1994 Redevelopment Act with the city holding the pen. The system was written for cities that build.

Fort Lawton is what it produces in a city that does not. The Army recommended closing the 34 acre Reserve Center in 2005. The city approved 500 homes. Twenty-one years later the Army still holds title, the second supplemental environmental review runs to 391 pages, and no one has built anything. Meanwhile a 1932 Albert Kahn factory sits on the sale list, a 1933 Art Deco block was named non-core, and the archives building at Sand Point empties when its replacement is built.

In 1996 Congress faced the same question at the Presidio of San Francisco and answered it differently. It refused to give the land to the city and refused to auction it, and chartered a federal trust with a board and a deadline to pay for itself. The trust hit the deadline eight years early. That is the proposal here, applied to Seattle. The anchor file is The Fort Lawton Trust, and the mechanism sits on Federal Levers as the ninth lever.

  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Fort Lawton Trust THE ASK TRANSFER STALLED 34 acres on the Magnolia bluff, recommended for closure in 2005, and the Army still holds title. The city's plan for 500 homes is on its third environmental document and has produced none of them. Congress refused to hand the Presidio to San Francisco in 1996 and built a trust that paid for itself by 2005. Do that here.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Ford Plant on the Duwamish FOR SALE NOW ON THE SALE LIST Albert Kahn designed it and Ford built cars in it from 1932. The General Services Administration has 422,195 square feet of it on the accelerated disposition list, posted May 2025 and still listed. It is also the best building in this city for manufacturing the materials a design standard requires.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Jackson Building TOWER FLAGGED, LIST WITHDRAWN Thirty-seven stories and about 915,000 square feet of 1974 concrete on Second Avenue, named for the senator whose 1970 law let Seattle take 391 acres of Fort Lawton for free. Listed as non-core in March 2025 and the list came down the next day.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS 909 First Avenue ART DECO FLAGGED A full city block from 1933 with cast aluminum spandrels carrying federal agency seals, terra cotta floors, bronze urns, and a postal lobby in stained oak that has barely changed. On the National Register since 1979 and named in the same non-core coverage.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Nakamura Courthouse TERRA COTTA IN USE Gilbert Stanley Underwood, 1940, a concrete frame clad in terra cotta plate, named in 2001 for a Medal of Honor recipient this country had interned. The clearest demonstration in Seattle of the material the ornament trade stopped making.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS Sand Point LAKEFRONT ARCHIVES MOVING About 100 acres of NOAA lakefront with nine buildings and a vessel pier, plus the 1946 National Archives building the government approved selling in 2020 before the public reversed it. The replacement archives facility goes into design this summer.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS Pier 36 INVESTMENT NOT SURPLUS The Coast Guard is spending $323 million to home-port three Polar Security Cutters and the Healy here, and in April 2026 dropped its plan to take the Port's Pier 46 to do it. The best federal news on this waterfront in a decade.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Beacon Hill Campus VA NOT SURPLUS Forty-four acres and the largest hospital in the VA Puget Sound system, with more than 600,000 patient visits in 2022 and a $122 million research building from 2019. Not for sale, which makes it a construction-standard question instead of a land question.
  • ⚖ MEMBERS The Locks ARMY CORPS NOT SURPLUS The busiest lock complex in the country by vessel count, opened in 1917, carrying $1.2 billion a year in economic activity on gates that had to be replaced at 105 years old. Federal ownership is why the city cannot sell them and cannot fix them.
